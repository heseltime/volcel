What I care about most these days wouldn't do well on Instagram or LinkedIn. It is quieter than that, and more local: church music, a choir, a theatre group, Altmünster on the Traunsee, where I grew up, and a slow question about what a life is for.

By day I'm a software engineer at Dynatrace, working in observability: the craft of making large, busy systems legible, so that the people who run them can see what is actually happening. I came to it by a winding road, from literature and film in New York to software engineering and AI at home in Linz, and I still think the humanities part of that road is the more important half.[^road]

## Why "volcel"

The name is short for *voluntarily celibate*. I mean it lightly, and not as a statement. It points at a journey I'm on, toward a religious community and a way of living that takes prayer and work equally seriously. I don't yet know exactly where that path ends. This blog is one place where I walk it out loud, with my roots still in technical work.

## The lake

Altmünster was once simply Münster, named for an abbey recorded there in 909 that did not last long. The parish church is still dedicated to St. Benedict.[^minster] In Traunkirchen, the next village along the shore, the Jesuits held the old convent from 1622 until the order was suppressed in 1773, and their church still has the Fischerkanzel, a pulpit with the apostles in a boat hauling up a net full of fish. I grew up between those two churches. The small sun that marks this blog is a nod to the Jesuits' emblem.

## A bus ride

In C.S. Lewis's *The Great Divorce*, the narrator waits for a bus in a grey town where it is always dusk and always raining. The bus lifts off and carries its passengers to the foothills of a country so much more real than they are that the grass hurts their feet.[^lewis] Most of them go back. What stays with me is less the destination than the ride: an in-between hour where you have left one place and not yet arrived at the other, and can see both a little more clearly.

That is roughly what I want these pages to be. Not a finished argument, but a liminal place between work, creative time and spiritual life. The writing here is meant to declutter: to streamline how I think about important work, technical and otherwise, so that there is more room for creativity and for inner space.

I keep noticing that my day job and this inner work ask similar questions. What is signal and what is noise? Where does my attention actually go? A good dashboard leaves things out so that the one thing that matters stands out; a bad one adds a hundred panels and makes you feel informed while you understand less. The old practice of the examen, an honest look back over the day to notice what moved you and what you'd rather not see, sounds a lot like a review of what a system has been doing while nobody was watching.

And then the stranger question: could the tools themselves help? Can the habits of observability (instrument, look, simplify, look again) be turned toward decluttering a life, or does that just add another dashboard? I don't know yet.
