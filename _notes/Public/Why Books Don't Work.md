Andy Matuschak's essay [[Why books don't work::https://andymatuschak.org/books/]] asks why explanatory non-fiction so often leaves readers without real understanding. Books and lectures, he argues, lean on an implicit model in which knowledge passes from author to reader by absorption.[^transmission] Understanding doesn't work that way: it comes from what the reader does with the ideas. So the essay asks what a medium built around how people actually learn would look like.

## Reading horizontally

His own [[working notes::https://notes.andymatuschak.org/]] are one answer, at least in their form. Following a link there doesn't replace the note you're reading. The linked note opens beside it, to the right, and the earlier note stays in view. Reading becomes a row of notes across the screen: the path you took, with each step still visible.

A book is linear: one line, set by the author. Here the line is the reader's. You choose which link to follow, and you can see where you came from while you read on. That is a small but real shift of the work from author to reader, which is the shift the essay asks for.

## Branching

The row isn't fixed. Go back to an earlier note, follow a different link, and everything to the right of it falls away; the path branches from there. Reading becomes a series of choices you can undo, closer to thinking than to listening.

It only works because of how the notes are written. Matuschak calls them [[evergreen notes::https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes]]: small, each about one idea, and densely linked, so that any note can be the next step in many different paths.

## Here

This site borrows that reading model for my own notes; [[Stacked Notes]] describes how it behaves. His notes are his research environment, not a published tool, so nothing here is his code, only the idea.[^code] What I add is the picture of topics and threads crossing, described in [[A Lattice, Not a Garden]].
